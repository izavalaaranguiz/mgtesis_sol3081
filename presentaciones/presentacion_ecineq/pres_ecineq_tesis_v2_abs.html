Intergenerational sources of homogeneity and occupational segregation in contact networks

Origin, destination and tie closeness in Santiago, Chile

Second Conference ECINEQ–LAC Meeting 28-29 October 2026, Montevideo.
ignacio.zavala@uc.cl

Ignacio Zavala Aránguiz & Gabriel Otero

Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, Universidad Diego Portales

Personal networks do not just reflect inequality: they help reproduce it

  • Information, job opportunities and support circulate through personal networks (Burt, 2010; Granovetter, 2010; Van Tubergen & Volker, 2015), so their socioeconomic composition shapes which advantages accumulate and which do not (Bourdieu, 1986; DiMaggio & Garip, 2012; Lin, 2001).
  • People tend to associate with those who resemble them in occupation and sociodemographic traits, especially in unequal and segregated contexts (McPherson et al., 2001; Otero et al., 2021).
  • This links to a central question in stratification: how much of the position people attain, and of the resources attached to it, traces back to social origin. Access to social capital depends on initial positions, that is, on parental resources, and not only on current position (Goldman, 2022; Lin, 1999).

Why Chile? High inequality, low intergenerational mobility, and marked residential and educational segregation (Méndez & Gayo, 2019; Torche, 2005, 2014): a setting where contact between classes is structurally restricted.

To what extent origin and destination positions are associated with the homogeneity and occupational segregation of contact networks in Santiago, and how does this relationship differ between close ties and acquaintances?

The literature anchors similarity in current position; social origin remains unexamined

  • Studies of network composition model similarity from a single reference: ego’s current occupation, or destination (Espinosa-Rada et al., 2026; Otero et al., 2021).
  • Social origin organizes the earliest and most durable ties (extended family, childhood neighborhood, school), stratified by the occupation of the household of upbringing (Blau, 1977; Feld, 1981; Lareau, 2011). Those contacts are not necessarily erased by later changes in position (Newman, 2008; Wellman, 2018).
  • Do people know more people close to their social origin or to their current position, and what does each case reveal about how stratification operates in networks?

Homogeneity and segregation: two levels of analysis

  • Homogeneity operates at the level of the ego: how similar are the people each person reports knowing, once group sizes and sociability are accounted for.
  • Segregation operates at the level of access to groups: whether the members of an occupation are known by many people or only by a few circles (DiPrete et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2006). Those circles are formed by who is similar to whom, so segregation inevitably captures some homogeneity.
  • Studies that use both concepts do not distinguish their levels. We keep them apart and link them through concentration: if the chance of knowing someone in occupation k depends on how close ego is to k, contacts pile up among nearby egos and counts become overdispersed (\(\omega > 1\)). The part of \(\omega\) absorbed by distance shows how much access-level segregation is organized around ego-level reference points (Baum, 2025).

Gabriel · ~2 min. Do NOT say “they are not the same”. The point is about LEVELS of analysis. Homogeneity is a property read from the ego: whom a person reports knowing. Segregation is read at the level of access to groups: how concentrated, across the population, is the knowledge of members of an occupation. Because that concentration is produced by who is similar to whom, segregation inevitably captures some homogeneity. The studies that have used both concepts do not distinguish these levels, and that is what we correct: we keep both, separate their levels, and connect them through concentration. The figure is illustrative (simulated, not data): both panels have the same average number of contacts in occupation k, but in the second they are concentrated in few egos. Overdispersion ω above 1 is our measure of that concentration; it reflects segregation to the extent that the concentration follows barriers of access, which is why we ask how much of it distance explains. Our findings relate the two levels: the coefficients of distance are ego-level (homogeneity); the explained share of ω is access-level (segregation). If asked: if segregation is ordered by destination, barriers depend on the position attained; if by origin, they persist beyond changes in position (Blau, 1977; Otero et al., 2021).

Three questions for Santiago

Why Chile is informative: if similarity to origin and to destination is identifiable where barriers between classes are already pronounced, it is likely a general feature of stratified societies (Contreras et al., 2019; Méndez & Gayo, 2019; Torche, 2014).

1. Homogeneity. Does ego’s origin keep a role in whom they know, net of their current destination? And how do contacts resemble destination and origin?

2. Segregation. Are high- and low-status occupations more segregated in their access than middle-status ones? How much of that segregation does status similarity explain, and is it similarity to origin or to destination?

3. Tie closeness. Do both patterns get stronger among close ties (close friends and family) than among acquaintances?

Original data: 1,124 people report whom they know in 27 occupations

  • Social Survey of Class, Networks and Political Attitudes (2025): Metropolitan Region of Santiago, 41 communes; probabilistic, multistage, stratified sample; face-to-face; N = 1,124.
  • Position Generator (Lin & Dumin, 1986) with 27 occupations: “How many people do you know who work as…?” and, of them, “how many are close friends or family?” Result: 30,348 ego–occupation dyads, i.e. aggregated relational data (Zheng et al., 2006).
  • Counts: \(c_{ik}\) = all contacts of ego \(i\) in occupation \(k\);
  • \(f_{ik}\) = close ties (close friends or family);
  • \(w_{ik} = c_{ik} - f_{ik}\) = acquaintances.
  • Status: ISEI-08 (Ganzeboom et al., 1992) of each occupation, in three levels: high (≥ 66), middle (41–65) and low (≤ 40).

Ignacio · ~1.5 min. Sampling: 10 territorial strata, then census zones, dwellings, and the adult with the most recent birthday in each household. The instrument does not enumerate ties one by one: it asks how many people ego knows in each occupation (aggregated relational data) and, of those, how many are close friends or family. We model close ties and acquaintances separately; both are counts with a high share of zeros (75.9% and 74.9%), because people often know nobody in occupations far from their own. The three ISEI levels are used later to read the results by status.

Distance between ego and each occupation, in ISEI points

  • Two reference positions for each ego \(i\): destination (ISEI of own occupation) and social origin (ISEI of the household head when ego was 15).

\[D^{\text{dest}}_{ik}=\frac{|\mathrm{ISEI}_k-\mathrm{ISEI}_i|}{10},\qquad D^{\text{orig}}_{ik}=\frac{|\mathrm{ISEI}_k-\mathrm{ISEI}_{i}^{\,15}|}{10}\]

From baseline segregation to the covariate model

  • Baseline. If the propensity to know occupation \(k\) varies across egos, counts are overdispersed by a factor \(\omega_k\): \(\omega_k=1\) is random mixing, \(\omega_k>1\) is segregation (DiPrete et al., 2011; Zheng et al., 2006).
  • Covariate model (Baum, 2025): the propensity gets an explained part \(\lambda_{ik}\); the overdispersion left is \(\omega'_k\):

\[\log\mu_{ik}=\alpha_i+\log m_k+\pi_k+\lambda_{ik}\]

  • \(\lambda_{ik}\) has two parts:

\[\lambda_{ik}=\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{l}\delta_{lk}X_{li}}_{\text{ego traits}}+\underbrace{\theta_{ik}}_{\text{two distances}}\]

  • Ego traits: sex, education and age.
  • Distances: absolute distance from destination and from origin, one \(\beta\) each per tie type, common to all occupations.

  • Since occupation size and sociability are already accounted for, each \(\beta\) is homogeneity relative to contact opportunity.

  • Bayesian estimation in Stan (Carpenter et al., 2017) via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (Baum, 2025; DiMaggio & Garip, 2012; Zheng et al., 2006), separately for close ties and acquaintances.

Ignacio · ~2.5 min. First the baseline, in one breath (this is the idea Gabriel showed with the simulated histograms): if everybody had the same chance of knowing anyone, counts would be Poisson; when the chance varies across people, the variance exceeds the mean by a factor ω, and the excess over 1 is segregation. Then the covariate model splits the propensity into a part explained by observed characteristics and a residual. Two kinds of explanatory terms: individual traits of ego (sex, education, age; their effects can differ by occupation) and the two absolute distances, from destination and from origin (their betas are common to all occupations). Because both are in the model, comparing ω with ω′ tells us how much segregation is associated with them. Estimation is Bayesian (Stan, four chains of 2,000 iterations, no divergences, R-hat ≤ 1.01); we report posterior means and 95% credible intervals. If asked about priors: weakly informative, in the appendix of the report.

Two quantities summarize how much the covariates explain

  • Explained proportion (Baum, 2025; McCormick et al., 2010):

\[\omega^{\text{prop}}_k=\frac{\omega_k-\omega'_k}{\omega_k-1}\]

From 0 (nothing explained) to 1 (everything). It is not how segregated \(k\) is, but how much of its segregation the covariates account for.

  • Unique contribution of a block \(X_b\) (e.g. distance to destination, both legs):

\[\Delta\omega^{\text{prop}}_k(X_b)=\omega^{\text{prop}}_{k,\text{full}}-\omega^{\text{prop}}_{k,\text{without }X_b}\]

Destination against origin: which anchor adds more?

Preliminary answers: distance anchors contacts, and segregation is only partly explained

1. Homogeneity. Contacts fall with status distance from both destination and origin. Destination weighs more only among close ties; for acquaintances the two anchors are indistinguishable.

2. Segregation. Every occupation is segregated, most at both ends of the hierarchy among close ties. Distance and ego traits explain about 20% (close ties) and 10% (acquaintances), mostly at the top; destination contributes more than origin only among close ties.

3. Tie closeness. Close ties are more homogeneous (steeper decline from both anchors) and their segregation is more explained by distance and traits.

If status similarity explains little, access barriers have other sources

  • The middle strata as a point of contact. The U-shape and the low segregation of everyday trades and services echo Chilean evidence that middle classes hold the most heterogeneous networks and may bridge the “edges” of the class structure (Burt, 2010; Otero et al., 2021; Otero et al., 2026). But not the whole stratum and specially not in the same layers of personal networks.

  • Barriers need not operate through similarity of status: shared settings such as residential and school segregation (Méndez & Gayo, 2019), the organizations where ties are formed (Feld, 1981), class-specific socialization (Lareau, 2011) and the structure of opportunities itself (Blau, 1977; McPherson et al., 2001).

  • Close ties are where trust, information and resources circulate. If status similarity and origin persist more there, they are a plausible channel for the transmission of advantages through social capital

  • Implication. A literature that reads segregation as similarity in status (by origin or destination) can underestimate these other sources.

Ignacio or Gabriel · ~1.5 min, closing. This is the interpretive step; keep it as hypotheses, not as findings. (1) Middle classes: the U-shape in close ties matches the Chilean evidence from Otero et al. 2021 (class homogeneity is high at the top and bottom, lower in the middle) and from the longitudinal work on class stability and mobility (lower-middle-class stable individuals have less homogeneous networks). Our occupational result adds nuance: the low-segregation middle occupations are trades and services in everyday use (electricians, mechanics, accountants, security guards); middle-status occupations such as secondary and primary teachers, carabineros and nurses are highly overdispersed. (2) The point of the whole talk: if distance in status, from origin and destination, plus sex, education and age explain only about 20% of the excess overdispersion among close ties and 10% among acquaintances, then access to occupations is organized by something else: where people live and study, which organizations bring them together, how classes socialize, and how large each group is. A literature that equates segregation with similarity in status may therefore underestimate those sources. (3) Be explicit about the limits: preliminary, exploratory, covariates still to be extended (income, children and partner, municipality size), and the residual may also reflect measurement of ISEI and of origin.

Appendix A. Why one coefficient per anchor: “up” versus “down” cannot be separated here

  • The issue. Signed distance to an occupation equals (distance up) minus (distance down) and is additive in ego’s position and in the occupation. Ego-specific sociability and occupation-specific baselines already absorb those terms.
  • What is identified is the average slope of the decline, \(\gamma=(\beta^{+}+\beta^{-})/2\), which is what the symmetric specification estimates. The difference between the upward and downward slopes is determined by the prior, not by the data.
  • Consequence. We do not claim that contacts fall faster upward than downward (a “social ceiling”); that question needs a different design. Estimates are unchanged in substance: the symmetric coefficients match the average of the earlier up/down estimates, and \(\omega^{\text{prop}}\) correlates 0.9998 across specifications.

Caveat on close ties. They mix family and close friends; part of their homogeneity may be kinship (shared origin) rather than choice. Read the close-tie gap as an upper bound on selective similarity.

Appendix B. Reading the explained share with care

  • Levels are not comparable. \(\omega\) scales with the average number of contacts: we compare close ties and acquaintances, and occupations of different size, through the ratio \(\omega^{\text{prop}}\), never through \(\omega\) itself.
  • Unstable when \(\omega\) is near 1. The denominator \(\omega-1\) is small for occupations close to random mixing (electricians, 1.11 in close ties); their \(\omega^{\text{prop}}\) can be large or negative with little substantive meaning. Negative values are read as “nothing explained”.
  • Possible geometric component. Absolute distance folds the ISEI scale: for occupations at the extremes, distance varies with ego’s position along the whole range; for middle occupations it varies less. Distance can therefore explain more of the dispersion at the top for geometric reasons. Pending check: the variance of distance across egos by occupation against \(\omega^{\text{prop}}\).

Ignacio · for Q&A only. This is the “mechanical relation” flank. Three points. (1) ω is not a pure measure of segregation: it scales with mean counts, so we never compare levels across tie types or occupations; we use ω_prop and Δω_prop. (2) ω_prop = (ω − ω′)/(ω − 1) divides by the excess; where the excess is tiny the ratio is noisy; we report it but do not interpret single occupations near 1. (3) Honest limit: because distance is absolute and ISEI is a single scale, occupations at the extremes get more variation in distance across egos than middle ones; part of “more explained at the top” could be geometry rather than substance. We have not yet checked it; the check is the correlation between the variance of distance across egos for each occupation and its ω_prop, and a sensitivity with a different distance (e.g., rank-based). Note that the bottom occupations are also at an extreme and are NOT well explained, so geometry cannot be the whole story.
